# Gold-Standard Production Prompt Example

Use this example as a quality benchmark for final production prompts involving dialogue, strict timing, references, continuous camera movement, a reveal, audio, and continuity. Do not copy its creative details into unrelated videos. Learn the structural discipline and specificity.

## Why this example is strong

- Maps every reference to a concrete production responsibility.
- Establishes style and physical capture language before motion.
- Separates pronunciation, voice treatment, timeline, performance, camera, continuity, audio, and negative constraints.
- Uses exact time windows for dramatic beats.
- Ties the camera reveal to one exact spoken word.
- Makes the opening and reveal one continuous physical environment, preventing magical transformation.
- Repeats exact dialogue in a dedicated block to reduce paraphrase drift.
- Gives performers playable restraint rather than vague emotional adjectives.
- Uses a focused `AVOID` list based on actual likely failures.

---

**20–24 SECONDS — 16:9 — PHOTOREALISTIC LIVE ACTION — 1960s RETRO-FUTURIST STYLE**

Reference 1 = ASTRONAUT 1
Reference 2 = ASTRONAUT 2
Reference 3 = VINTAGE PORTABLE COMPUTER / PROTOTYPE UI
Reference 4 = 1960s TV SOUNDSTAGE WITH ROVER SET

Maintain the exact visual identity of References 1 and 2 throughout.

Reference 3 establishes the physical old-fashioned computer and the prototype UI shown on its screen.

Reference 4 establishes the entire physical environment: the rover, fake planetary terrain, studio floor, cameras, crew, lighting equipment, and full 1960s soundstage.

Reference 1 and Reference 2 are the only speaking characters.

No duplicate astronauts.
No identity swapping.
No additional astronauts.

---

## STYLE

Absolute live-action photographic realism.

The entire video feels physically filmed in the late 1960s.

Retro-futurist space exploration aesthetic.

The opening genuinely looks like two astronauts operating a planetary rover on another world.

The final reveal shows that the entire environment is actually a practical television set inside a 1960s soundstage.

Dry, understated comedy.

Play everything completely straight.

Real human faces.

Natural skin texture.

Real astronaut fabric and hardware.

Analog controls.

Chunky mechanical equipment.

Soft vintage optics.

Visible but subtle film grain.

Gentle highlight bloom.

Slight halation around practical lights.

Muted period color reproduction.

No modern digital sharpness.

No glossy CGI appearance.

---

## PRONUNCIATION — ABSOLUTE PRIORITY

Whenever Figma is spoken, pronounce it:

**FIG-muh**

Two syllables.

First syllable sounds exactly like the English word **fig**.

Second syllable is a short relaxed **muh**.

The spoken result must sound naturally like:

**FIG-muh**

Do not pronounce it:

Figba,
Figa,
Sigma,
Fig-mah.

In dialogue, the brand is written phonetically as **FIG-muh** to preserve correct pronunciation.

---

## VOICE / HELMET AUDIO

Both astronauts are wearing helmets.

Their voices sound like they are being transmitted through a believable 1960s spacecraft radio system.

Slightly muffled.

Narrow analog radio frequency range.

Very light static.

Subtle compression.

Clear intelligibility.

Do not heavily distort the dialogue.

The voices remain natural and human.

Casual workplace conversation inside a ridiculous space-mission context.

No robotic voices.

No announcer delivery.

---

## 0–6 SECONDS — THE NEW TOOL

Begin in a tight two-shot inside the rover from Reference 4.

Frame tightly enough that the audience cannot yet see the surrounding soundstage.

Reference 1 and Reference 2 sit side-by-side inside the rover.

The rover interior feels convincingly 1960s retro-futurist:

painted metal panels,
toggle switches,
mechanical gauges,
indicator lights,
visible bolts,
thick wiring,
simple utilitarian seats.

Through the rover windows, only convincing fake planetary terrain is visible.

Do NOT reveal any studio equipment yet.

Reference 1 holds Reference 3 on their lap.

Reference 3 is a chunky old-fashioned portable computer.

Heavy beige or off-white casing.

Small built-in CRT-style display.

Large keyboard.

Physical buttons.

Subtle wear.

No futuristic tablet.

No modern laptop.

Reference 1 looks down at the computer and says exactly:

**“Hey, I tried this new prototyping tool instead of FIG-muh.”**

Reference 2 looks toward the screen.

Reference 2:

**“Does it use our components?”**

Reference 1:

**“No.”**

---

## 6–11 SECONDS — TOKENS

Reference 2 keeps looking at Reference 3.

Reference 2:

**“Our tokens?”**

Reference 1:

**“No.”**

Brief silence.

Reference 2 slowly looks back toward Reference 1.

No exaggerated facial reaction.

Keep the performance sincere and slightly puzzled.

---

## 11–16 SECONDS — THE QUESTION

Reference 2 asks:

**“So it’s fake?”**

Deliver the line completely straight.

Do not perform it as a punchline.

At the exact word:

**“fake”**

the camera begins a very slow, smooth physical dolly backward.

No cut.

No jump.

No digital zoom.

The rover still appears convincing for the first moment of the move.

---

## 16–23 SECONDS — SOUNDSTAGE REVEAL

Continue pulling backward in one uninterrupted move.

Reference 4 controls the entire revealed environment.

As the camera retreats, gradually expose the boundaries of the rover set.

First reveal:

the edge of a rover wall,
the end of the fake planetary ground,
a lighting stand at the edge of frame.

Continue moving backward.

Reveal more of the 1960s TV studio:

large period television cameras on heavy rolling pedestals,
camera operators wearing headsets,
boom microphone operators,
analog sound technicians,
large tungsten Fresnel lights,
metal lighting stands,
thick black cables,
sandbags,
wooden apple boxes,
production carts,
analog monitors,
painted backdrop flats,
unfinished scenery backs,
stagehands,
a director watching playback.

All crew members wear believable late-1960s production clothing.

No modern cameras.

No LED panels.

No laptops.

No smartphones.

No contemporary clothing.

The fake planet occupies only a small central portion of the much larger studio.

The clear contrast must become obvious:

**convincing space mission up close → obviously constructed television set from far away.**

Reference 1 and Reference 2 remain seated inside the rover.

They never acknowledge the studio.

Reference 1 looks back down at Reference 3.

Reference 1:

**“Yeah…”**

Hold for one short beat on the fully revealed 1960s soundstage.

**HARD CUT TO BLACK.**

No fade.

---

Do not paraphrase.

Do not add words.

Do not remove words.

Reference 1:

**“Hey, I tried this new prototyping tool instead of FIG-muh.”**

Reference 2:

**“Does it use our components?”**

Reference 1:

**“No.”**

Reference 2:

**“Our tokens?”**

Reference 1:

**“No.”**

Reference 2:

**“So it’s fake?”**

Reference 1:

**“Yeah…”**

---

## PERFORMANCE

Both astronauts behave like serious professionals.

Dry.

Natural.

Underplayed.

No exaggerated reactions.

No smiling at the joke.

No winking.

No looking into camera.

Natural breathing.

Small helmeted head movements.

Subtle eye movements visible through visors.

The conversation should feel like two coworkers casually discussing a prototyping workflow.

---

## CAMERA

ONE CONTINUOUS SHOT.

Opening:

tight two-shot inside the rover.

Camera mostly stable.

On **“So it’s fake?”**

begin a slow continuous physical dolly backward.

The camera travels straight backward through the open front-facing side of the practical rover set and into the studio.

Reference 4 provides a clear central camera corridor for this move.

Reveal the illusion progressively.

Do not immediately jump to the wide shot.

No orbit.

No pan away from the astronauts.

No whip pan.

No digital zoom.

No cut before the final hard cut.

The final frame is a wide view of the entire soundstage with the rover now visibly small inside the larger production environment.

---

## ENVIRONMENT CONTINUITY

Reference 4 is one continuous physical environment.

The rover and soundstage are NOT two different locations.

The rover is a practical set physically sitting inside the studio from the beginning.

The planetary landscape is also practical scenery inside the studio.

Nothing transforms.

Nothing appears magically.

The camera simply reveals more of the existing environment.

The opening hides the studio through framing.

The final pullback exposes it.

---

## AUDIO

Astronaut dialogue remains the primary audio.

Subtle 1960s helmet-radio coloration.

Very light static.

Faint rover mechanical hum.

Quiet ventilation.

Small physical switch sounds.

As the studio becomes visible, gradually introduce subtle production-floor ambience:

crew footsteps,
camera mechanism noise,
cable movement,
quiet equipment handling.

No loud crew chatter.

No modern electronic sounds.

No comedy sound effects.

No dramatic music sting.

---

## COMEDIC PRIORITY

The astronauts are discussing a prototype that appears convincing but does not use their real components or tokens.

Reference 2 asks:

**“So it’s fake?”**

At that exact moment, the audience begins discovering that the astronauts’ entire space mission is also fake.

The verbal joke and visual reveal happen together.

Do not explain this.

Do not have either astronaut acknowledge the studio.

Reference 1 simply answers:

**“Yeah…”**

The dry acceptance and immediate ending are the punchline.

---

## AVOID

incorrect pronunciation of FIG-muh, Figba, Figa, Sigma, Fig-mah, altered dialogue, paraphrased dialogue, duplicate astronauts, identity swapping, extra astronauts, modern sci-fi aesthetic, sleek spaceship interiors, modern laptops, tablets, smartphones, LED screens, holograms, floating UI, modern cinema cameras, LED studio lights, contemporary clothing, green screens, LED volume walls, obvious studio equipment visible in the opening, instant environment transformation, cuts during the reveal, exaggerated acting, astronauts laughing at the joke, astronauts acknowledging the studio, robotic voices, heavy radio distortion, unintelligible dialogue, subtitles, captions, text overlays, watermarks.
