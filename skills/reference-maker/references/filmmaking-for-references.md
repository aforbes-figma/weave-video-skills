# Filmmaking Principles for Reference Images

Use these rules to turn a static reference into a production-ready starting frame or coverage system.

## Story and point of view

- State the emotional job of the shot before choosing camera placement.
- Choose an objective frame when the audience should observe geography or behavior neutrally.
- Choose a subjective frame when the audience should share one character's emotional position.
- Identify whose point of view owns each shot. Do not create a frame that is visually intimate with several competing viewpoints at once.
- Use camera height deliberately: near eyeline for direct connection, lower for greater weight or power, higher for diminished strength or vulnerability.

## Geography and screen logic

- Establish the room, path, entrances, exits, fixed landmarks, and relative subject positions before designing coverage.
- Define a dominant screen axis and preserve left/right relationships unless an axis crossing is motivated and shown.
- Preserve eyeline direction and height across matching shots.
- Leave entry room, exit room, look room, and action room in the direction the subject will move or look.
- Use a wide or moving geography anchor when the audience would otherwise lose track of the space.

## Blocking before camera

- Define where the subject begins, what motivates movement, the path taken, contact points, and the settled end position.
- Place the camera after blocking is understandable. Let performance and story motivate movement rather than adding a decorative move.
- Give the first frame a balanced, physically stable pose that can naturally initiate the planned action.
- Avoid limbs, props, hair, dust, and motion blur that imply movement opposite the prompt.

## Lens and perspective continuity

- Describe camera distance and field-of-view character together. Focal length alone does not define perspective.
- Use wider perspectives when geography, speed, or parallax matters; keep critical faces and products away from distortion-prone edges.
- Use longer perspectives when compression, isolation, or graphic layering matters; ensure the environment still supplies enough depth for the planned motion.
- Keep lens family, camera height, horizon, and subject scale coherent across shots that should feel continuous.

## Lighting continuity

- Define the key source by direction, height, hardness, color, and motivation.
- Track practical sources, window direction, sun direction, shadow angle, reflection behavior, and ambient fill across reference frames.
- Keep the lighting physically plausible for the established set geometry.
- Use lighting changes only when story time, location, or a visible source motivates them.

## Coverage references

Create only the coverage anchors the edit or generation genuinely needs:

1. **Geography anchor** - establishes space, axis, positions, and light direction.
2. **Hero first frame** - exact starting composition for the primary shot.
3. **Reaction or subjective frame** - only when emotional interpretation depends on it.
4. **Insert or detail frame** - only when a product, prop state, hand contact, or reveal must read clearly.
5. **End-state frame** - only when the landing composition cannot safely vary.

Derive shot-specific frames from the same approved location, lighting, and character assets. Do not independently regenerate each shot from prose.

## Frame-to-motion readiness

Before approving a reference, verify:

- the subject has room to move in the intended direction;
- the camera has an unobstructed and physically plausible corridor;
- foreground and background layers create useful depth and parallax;
- no crop, pose, shadow, reflection, or environmental cue contradicts the motion;
- start and end states can connect within the available runtime;
- the final delivery aspect ratio preserves the important action and gaze space.
